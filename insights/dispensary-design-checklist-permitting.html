Dispensary design checklist: what cannabis operators need before permitting

For most cannabis operators, permitting is where a project's schedule is won or lost. The drawings that go to the city or state have to answer a reviewer's questions about security, access, accessibility and life safety, and every unanswered question costs weeks. This checklist follows the order in which those questions tend to come up. Rules differ by state and municipality, so confirm specifics with your licensing authority, your building department and your attorney. The sequence below is a way to organize your design so those conversations go faster.

1. Confirm the site, zoning and buffers first

Before any design work, confirm that the address is allowed for your license type. Many jurisdictions limit dispensaries by zoning district and require minimum distances from certain uses such as schools. Check parking requirements, hours of operation and any limits on exterior signage. A site that fails one of these tests is far cheaper to discover in week one than after a lease is signed and design fees have been spent.

2. Plan the controlled entry

Most programs expect customers to enter through a controlled point, check in, and be verified before reaching the sales floor. Design the sequence from the front door to the point of sale: a vestibule or reception area, space to queue, a clear sightline for staff checking identification, and door hardware that meets your jurisdiction's requirements. Leave enough room that a busy Saturday does not push a line out the door or into a restricted area.

3. Lay out the sales floor like retail that also happens to be regulated

Display cases, point-of-sale stations and budtender counters need enough width for a browsing customer and staff to work side by side without a bottleneck at checkout. Group products by category so customers can find what they want, and confirm how your jurisdiction regulates what can be on display and how. Plan for kiosks or online-order pickup if you expect them, and keep accessible counter heights and clear paths in mind from the start.

4. Locate product storage and the vault deliberately

Storage and vault areas usually have specific construction, access-control and location requirements. Keep them away from exterior walls and windows, route inventory movement so it does not cross public areas, and plan a secure receiving point for deliveries. Give waste handling its own consideration, since many programs have rules about how product waste is stored and disposed of.

5. Design camera coverage and lighting together

Surveillance requirements typically include full coverage of entries, the sales floor, storage areas and exterior approaches, with recordings kept in a secure location. Plan camera positions on the reflected ceiling plan alongside lighting, because glare and shadows can ruin otherwise good coverage. Decide early where the recording equipment will live and how it will be powered and cooled.

6. Resolve accessibility and life safety

Accessible routes, restrooms, counter heights and egress still apply to a dispensary, and they can conflict with security features. A locked partition that is excellent for security can block an exit path if it is placed carelessly. Coordinate occupancy load, exit widths, door hardware and fire separation with the security plan so that neither undermines the other. This is a common source of plan-review comments.

7. Check mechanical, odor and power needs

Dispensaries often need ventilation approaches that manage odor, along with reliable power and data for point-of-sale systems, security equipment and displays. Confirm the capacity of the existing building early, particularly in older tenant spaces and converted buildings, because upgrades can drive both cost and schedule.

8. Plan compliant signage and branding

Many jurisdictions restrict exterior signage and advertising, and some limit what can be visible from the street. Design the storefront, window treatments and sign package with those limits in mind so your brand shows up in materials, color and lighting instead of in elements a reviewer will ask you to remove.

9. Assemble a drawing set the reviewer can follow

A clear set typically includes a floor plan, a security plan showing camera and alarm locations, a life-safety plan, accessibility details and a finish and equipment schedule. Coordinate the architectural, electrical, mechanical and security drawings before submission, and ask whether your jurisdiction offers a pre-application meeting. Complete, consistent drawings are the most reliable way to avoid a resubmittal.

Build the timeline around two tracks

State licensing and local building permits generally run on separate tracks and can overlap. A design team that understands both can sequence drawings so neither track waits on the other. Ask your designer how they have handled approvals in your jurisdiction and what they would expect to be asked.
